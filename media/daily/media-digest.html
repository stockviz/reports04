The 22,000 Cr Insolvency Puzzle: Unraveling Subhash Chandra’s Case – Creditors, Haircuts, and Legal Battles

INDUSINDBK AXISBANK IDBI RBLBANK ZEELEARN HDFCBANK CORALFINAC DAMCAPITAL LICHSGFIN UNIONBANK

The ₹22,000 crore insolvency case against Subhash Chandra (ZEE Group promoter) revolves around personal guarantees for corporate loans, with LIC Housing Finance (₹1,322 crore exposure) and other lenders (IDBI, HDFC Bank, Axis Bank, Canara Bank, etc.) seeking recovery. The NCLT (National Company Law Tribunal) approved a resolution plan where Chandra’s ₹6.25 crore (from his declared net worth of ~₹37 crore) settles personal guarantees, while creditors retain claims against corporate assets.

Key Controversies & Legal Nuances:

  1. Creditor Divisions & Dissent:
    • 80% of creditors approved the plan, but 20% dissent, alleging fraudulent wealth dissipation—Chandra’s net worth allegedly plummeted from ₹4,000+ crore (2020) to ₹31 crore in 2026.
    • LIC Housing Finance received just ₹38 lakh (0.03% of their claim), sparking outrage. They retain rights over secured assets and may pursue further action.
    • HDFC Bank plans to appeal to NCLAT against the NCLT order.
  2. Legal & Procedural Complexities:
    • Personal insolvency (against Chandra) is duplicative—claims were first filed against his corporate entities (under IBC), then against his personal guarantee.
    • Courts ruled that >66% creditor approval (commercial wisdom) trumps dissent, citing the Essar Steel precedent (where courts reversed an auction, leading to backlash).
    • Fraud allegations: Dissenting creditors argue Chandra moved wealth overseas (e.g., ₹600 crore warrants to a promoter holding company) to avoid guarantees. Courts cannot investigate commercial fraud but may probe deliberate asset stripping.
  3. Is It a “99% Haircut”?
    • Optical illusion: The ₹6.25 crore payout is only for personal guarantees, not the full ₹22,000 crore debt. Creditors still have recourse to corporate assets.
    • Past precedents (e.g., Vijay Mallya’s 1991–92 case) saw 70–75% haircuts, but this case is distinct—Chandra’s net worth collapse and wealth movement are under scrutiny.
    • Banks’ stance: If no fraud is proven, they’ve secured minimum liquidation value. If wealth was siphoned, they may lose out.
  4. Broader Debate on Personal Guarantees:
    • Why do banks take them? In India, promoters commingle personal and corporate assets, making guarantees a collateral comfort.
    • Criticism: Banks should first exhaust corporate assets before targeting personal wealth. The IBC framework allows this, but ethical concerns persist.

What’s Next?

  • Dissenting creditors will appeal to NCLAT, focusing on:
    • Fraud investigation (wealth dissipation).
    • Loss of recourse to personal guarantees.
  • Courts may probe if Chandra deliberately reduced net worth to avoid liabilities.
  • Outcome hinges on:
    • Asset recovery from corporate entities.
    • Proving fraudulent transfers (if any).
    • NCLAT’s interpretation of IBC’s fraud provisions.

Counter-Narratives:

  • Pro-Chandra view: He settled guarantees fairly (₹6.25 crore), unlike Vijay Mallya, who faced harsher consequences.
  • Pro-Bank view: If no fraud, they’ve secured partial recovery; if fraud exists, they may lose more.

Final Takeaway: The case exposes gaps in IBC’s fraud provisions, creditor conflicts, and the ethics of personal guarantees in India’s corporate lending. The real battle is over **asset recovery vs. legal loopholes
