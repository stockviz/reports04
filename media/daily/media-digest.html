AAIB Recommends Stricter Pilot Training Checks & Safety Measures After Learjet 45XR Crash

SAIL BMWVENTLTD

The Aircraft Accident Investigation Bureau (AAIB) has recommended that the Directorate General of Civil Aviation (DGCA) conduct a one-time verification of all fixed-wing pilots’ training records, particularly for non-scheduled operators, to ensure compliance with mandatory simulator exercises, including stall recovery in landing configuration.

The recommendations follow the September 2023 crash of a VSR Ventures Learjet 45XR (VT-DBL) at Mumbai Airport, which injured all eight onboard. The accident was attributed to: - Unstabilized approach under marginal weather and high tailwind. - Pilot errors, including failure to monitor airspeed and delay in calling for a go-around. - Non-adherence to SOPs and undue commitment to land before a restricted time.

Key Safety Recommendations:

  1. DGCA Actions:
    • One-time audit of pilot training records for non-scheduled/general aviation operators.
    • Mandate stall recovery training in all flight phases (climb, cruise, approach, landing) during simulator sessions.
    • Direct operators to develop verifiable methodologies ensuring pilots complete all mandatory emergency training.
    • Strengthen internal audits and enforce SOP compliance, including immediate go-around for unstabilized approaches.
  2. Operator-Specific Measures:
    • VSR Ventures must enhance cockpit crew checklists and maintain accurate aircraft logs.
    • AAI (Airports Authority of India) should train ATCs to mitigate wake turbulence risks.

The report highlights systemic gaps in training oversight, SOP adherence, and documentation, urging stricter regulatory and operational controls to prevent similar incidents

Binance’s Know Your Agent (KYA) Framework: Securing AI Agents in Crypto Compliance

Binance’s Head of Global Policy, Stephen McWhorter, advocates for the Know Your Agent (KYA) framework—a complement to KYC—to address risks posed by AI agents acting on behalf of customers in crypto and finance. While KYC verifies human customers, KYA extends accountability to software agents by defining their identity, permissions, revocations, and authority, akin to a job description. The goal is not to grant AI legal personality but to preserve human accountability as agents perform tasks like analyzing data, prioritizing alerts, or even executing transactions.

Key opportunities include AI-driven efficiency in financial crime compliance (e.g., tracing funds, connecting fragmented data), but risks escalate when agents transition from analysis to action (e.g., altering records, initiating payments). Binance emphasizes separating intelligence from control, enforcing bounded autonomy (strict permission thresholds), and ensuring machine-speed analysis doesn’t equate to uncontrolled authority.

Liability remains unsettled, though Binance argues regulated firms—not software—bear responsibility for deployed systems. KYA provides evidence for accountability (e.g., tracking agent actions, authorizations, and mandate adherence) but isn’t a legal shield. Regulators must balance technology-neutral rules (focusing on outcomes, not tools) with proportionate safeguards, scaling controls as agents gain more authority (e.g., moving money vs. retrieving data). The framework aligns with the “bounded autonomy principle”, ensuring agents operate within enforceable, scalable boundaries to mitigate risks while enabling innovation
