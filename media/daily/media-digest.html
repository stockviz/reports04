Supreme Court Reprimands CCI for Six-Year Delay in Star-Sony Anti-Trust Appeal

BNALTD SUNSHINE

The Supreme Court criticized the Competition Commission of India (CCI) for its six-year delay in pursuing an appeal against Star India (now JioStar) and Sony Pictures Network over alleged anti-competitive practices in the TV sector. The court questioned why the CCI failed to even issue a notice or seek admission of its 2020 appeal against a 2019 Bombay High Court ruling, which had overturned the CCI’s investigation order.

The case originated in 2017, when a distributor accused the broadcasters of discriminatory pricing and distribution practices. The CCI initially ordered an investigation in 2018, but the Bombay High Court quashed it in 2019, citing insufficient prima facie evidence. The CCI’s delayed appeal (2020–2026)—pending for years due to bench changes and adjournments—was dismissed by the Supreme Court, though it kept the legal questions open.

The dispute highlights jurisdictional challenges for the CCI in regulating TV broadcasting, a sector also governed by sector-specific laws. The court’s reprimand underscores systemic inertia in enforcement, despite the case’s significance in defining competition law boundaries. JioStar (formerly Star India) and Sony remain uncommented, while the CCI’s inaction raises concerns over **regulatory efficiency

RBI’s Unconventional Approach to Tata Sons Listing Sparks Legal and Regulatory Debate

NSIL TATACAP

The Reserve Bank of India’s (RBI) recent communication to Tata Sons—based on the company’s internal board meeting proceedings—has ignited controversy over regulatory practices and potential overreach. Legal experts argue that regulators typically rely on formal compliance frameworks rather than private board discussions to enforce directives. While the RBI cited Section 45JA of the RBI Act (empowering it to issue directions on NBFC operations), critics like H.P. Ranina and Nitin Potdar contend that the regulator lacks authority to mandate a listing, as such provisions govern financial management, not ownership structures. The RBI’s stance risks legal challenges, they warn.

The debate splits opinions: Shriram Subramanian and Srinath Sridharan defend the RBI’s position, asserting compliance with Upper Layer NBFC regulations demands listing, with Tata Sons being the sole non-compliant entity. However, Noel Tata (Tata Trusts Chairman) opposes listing, fearing it could dilute the group’s philanthropic mission, while Jimmy Tata supports a potential share sale and Chandrasekaran’s reappointment. Any listing would require converting Tata Sons into a public limited company, needing 75% shareholder approval—a hurdle that could override RBI demands under the Companies Act, per Ranina.

The dispute also reflects internal Tata group divisions, with Noel Tata’s resistance framed by critics as an attempt to evade scrutiny, while Jimmy Tata accuses him of prioritizing secrecy over transparency. The RBI’s suo motu action—triggered by Tata Sons’ October 6 query—highlights tensions between regulatory oversight and corporate autonomy

The Psychology of Investing: How Mind Traps Undermine Returns

TIMESGTY MOKSH

Investor outcomes often diverge not because of different products or market conditions but due to behavioral biases. Five common mind traps—loss aversion, herd mentality, recency bias, overconfidence, and anchoring—lead investors to panic‑sell, chase trends, overtrade, and hold onto losing positions. To protect returns, set clear written goals, automate contributions (e.g., SIPs), limit portfolio checks, apply a 48‑hour cooling‑off rule before major moves, and seek a trusted sounding board. Consistent, patient, goal‑driven investing typically outperforms reactive, emotion‑driven decisions
