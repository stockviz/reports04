The 2030 U.S. Gas Crisis: How Overreliance on LNG Exposed Global Vulnerabilities

TIMESGTY TCNSBRANDS TRANSPEK

By October 2030, the United States faced its third major natural‑gas shock in eight years as shale output stalled, extreme weather spiked demand, and AI data‑centers gobbled fuel, while LNG exports to foreign buyers drained domestic supplies. Record‑low storage and a February polar vortex pushed Henry Hub prices above $10/MMBtu, rippling through the economy: fertilizer costs tripled, crops shrank, factories closed, and auto plants cut shifts, costing thousands of jobs. Politically, the crisis ignited a backlash against exports, culminating in the bipartisan Secure American Fuel for Everyone Act, which ordered a rapid export curtailment. This move strained Europe, Japan, and South Korea, already low on storage and dependent on U.S. LNG, while Russia and the Gulf could not fill the gap. The episode echoes past protectionist bans and reveals the folly of treating LNG’s flexibility as energy security; nations that had diversified into solar and storage fared better, whereas wealthier regions that clung to imported gas suffered the most. The 2030 crisis underscores the perils of relying on geopolitically vulnerable fossil‑fuel supply chains
