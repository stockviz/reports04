China’s Green Hydrogen Drive: A High‑Stakes Bet for Global Energy Futures

BCG

China is rapidly expanding its green hydrogen capacity, now holding about 250,000 t/yr—over twice the rest of the world’s total—and aims to reach 2 million t/yr by 2030. The surge is powered by abundant wind and solar, cheap electrolyzers (four times cheaper than Europe’s), and strong government subsidies. Projects like Envision’s $2.6 bn plant in Inner Mongolia convert excess renewable power into hydrogen and ammonia, addressing curtailment and creating new demand for hard‑to‑decarbonize sectors such as steel, cement, and chemicals. However, green hydrogen remains costlier than gray or blue variants, and demand‑side policies are needed to close the price gap. If successful, China could cement its leadership in clean‑tech and provide a viable path to decarbonizing heavy industry; failure would cast doubt on green hydrogen’s commercial viability worldwide

Fire Insurance Premiums Plunge 28% in Q1 Due to Aggressive Discounting

ICICIGI NIACL GODIGIT

In the first four months of the financial year (April–July), fire insurance premiums in India dropped 28% year-over-year, totaling ₹10,062 crore compared to ₹14,063 crore in the same period last year. The decline stems from deep discounting on large industrial risks, intensifying competition among insurers for corporate accounts.

The Insurance Regulatory and Development Authority of India (IRDAI) has flagged concerns over discounts as high as 99% on major industrial fire policies, warning that such pricing undermines insurers’ financial stability and underwriting discipline. The regulator emphasized that pricing for low-frequency, high-severity risks (where a single claim can far exceed premiums collected) must adhere to sound actuarial principles.

Major insurers reflected the downturn: - New India Assurance: Fire premiums fell to ₹1,681 crore (from ₹2,241 crore). - ICICI Lombard: Dropped to ₹1,248 crore (from ₹1,815 crore). - Go Digit: Declined to ₹223 crore (from ₹413 crore).

The trend raises alarms about profitability and risk management in the sector

Meta Under Pressure: Landmark Settlement Forces Teen Safety Reforms on Social Media

Meta has agreed to a historic settlement with 29 U.S. states (excluding New Mexico and Florida) to address teen addiction risks on its platforms, including Facebook and Instagram. The deal—worth up to $18 billion—requires sweeping changes like daily time limits, school-hour notification blocks, algorithmic feed restrictions, midnight-to-6 AM lockouts, and stricter age-gating to curb underage access. Additional measures include hiding likes to reduce dopamine-driven engagement and banning toxic beauty filters.

While critics argue the changes are optional by default (though parents can lock them in) and limited to U.S. teens, Meta’s move signals a shift in accountability for tech companies, weakening the long-used Section 230 defense that shields platforms from liability. The settlement also includes a $5.3 billion incentive for competitors like TikTok and YouTube to adopt similar limits—otherwise, Meta’s teen usage caps will tighten further (e.g., to one hour/day).

Financially, Meta frames the settlement as a strategic win, avoiding a potential $1.4 trillion lawsuit and existential risks like a teen ban. Advertising revenue may dip, but the company likely expects workarounds (e.g., premium ad placements before time limits kick in). Investors reacted positively, with shares rising ~1%.

Key concerns remain: - Loopholes: Meta’s history of adapting to restrictions (e.g., bypassing Apple’s privacy tracking limits) raises doubts about long-term compliance. - Global reach: Why limit reforms to the U.S.? - Effectiveness: Optional defaults may encourage teens to disable protections.

Despite flaws, the settlement marks progress in holding tech accountable, though critics argue it’s too little, too late—and Meta’s motives are self-serving. The pressure on rivals like TikTok (where teens spend 1h36m/day) could accelerate industry-wide reforms, but enforcement and unintended consequences (e.g., driving users to less-regulated platforms) loom
