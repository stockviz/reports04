Nvidia’s Crisis: The Race to Restore Jensen Huang Amid Scandal and Backlash

Four days after Jensen Huang resigned from Nvidia amid revelations of affairs—including ties to a woman suspected of links to China’s intelligence services—an unprecedented campaign has emerged to reinstate him as CEO. Over 180,200 people (including 8,800+ Nvidia employees and 94,000+ shareholders) have signed a “Stand With Jensen” petition, with high-profile supporters like Elon Musk, Tim Sweeney (Epic Games), and Donald Trump endorsing his return. Trump even suggested he wouldn’t oppose Huang’s reinstatement, though U.S. Treasury Secretary Scott Bessent urged an outsider CEO to signal a “dramatic shake-up.”

Key Divides & Risks

  • Supporters argue Huang’s leadership, deep employee loyalty, and critical relationships (e.g., with TSMC, Nvidia’s sole manufacturer of advanced chips) make him irreplaceable. Taiwan, where Huang is a cultural icon (“Jensanity”), fears his exit weakens its AI-driven economy.
  • Opponents—including governance experts and institutional investors—warn reinstating Huang prematurely could expose Nvidia to legal risks and shareholder lawsuits while investigations into his personal conduct and potential espionage ties (via Alice Huang) continue. U.S. intelligence is still assessing her role as a suspected intelligence asset.

Board’s Stance & Market Reaction

  • Nvidia’s board is not considering Huang’s return and is actively searching for a successor. Shares dropped 3.7% ahead of Q3 earnings.
  • Taiwan’s reaction: Taipei 101 was lit up with messages like “Taiwan Loves Jensen,” reflecting his outsized influence. Locals compare his potential return to Steve Jobs’ comeback at Apple.
  • Investor split: While some (e.g., Gavin Baker of Atreides) urge patience, large institutions may resist due to uncertainty.

Unresolved Questions

  • Will the board prioritize stability (Huang’s return) or risk mitigation (outsider CEO)?
  • How will China-U.S. tensions (given espionage allegations) impact Nvidia’s global operations?
  • Can Huang’s cultural and business legacy outweigh the scandal, or is this a turning point for the company?

The debate tests whether Nvidia’s future hinges on loyalty to its founder or a clean break amid growing scrutiny

Amazon Faces First North American Union Contract in Canada: A Battle Over Labor Rights and Arbitration

Amazon workers at its Delta, British Columbia warehouse have secured North America’s first union contract with the company, imposed by an arbitrator between Unifor and Amazon. The agreement, running until November 13, 2029, covers over 700 workers and includes terms on wages, scheduling, layoffs, and benefits—though Amazon argues the terms are similar to existing conditions.

Key Controversies: - Mandatory Unionization: The contract forces all employees to join Unifor and pay up to C$1,064 ($744 USD) in dues annually, potentially reducing take-home pay, according to Amazon. - Arbitration Without Ratification: Amazon claims workers weren’t given a vote on the contract, calling the process “improper” and “unfair.” Some employees are also pushing to decertify the union. - Legal Challenges: Amazon filed for judicial review in the British Columbia Supreme Court to block the contract, citing a pending decertification effort. The union’s certification came via remedial certification after allegations of Amazon’s anti-union tactics, including a hiring spree to delay certification.

Broader Context: This follows Amazon’s first U.S. unionization in Staten Island (2022), though negotiations remain stalled. Amazon opposes third-party unions, advocating for direct worker input, while Unifor celebrates the “historic win” for labor rights.

Amazon’s Stance: The company insists on direct negotiations without unions or dues, calling the arbitrated contract “defying common sense.” Unifor and workers argue it’s a rare victory against corporate resistance
