Big Food Pushes Back Against India’s Front‑of‑Pack Nutrition Warning Labels

NESTLEIND

India’s food‑safety regulator abandoned plans for colorful front‑of‑pack health warnings after intense lobbying by multinational firms such as Coca‑Cola and Nestlé. Companies argued that red‑flag labels are confusing, ineffective and would unfairly brand many traditional products as “high‑risk.” Instead, the regulator proposed a black‑and‑white nutrition table, a move now being reviewed by the Supreme Court following a petition by public‑health activists. Evidence from Reuters recordings shows industry executives downplaying the impact of warning symbols and urging focus on portion control. While firms voluntarily use traffic‑light labels in Europe, they resist similar standards in India, fearing that a majority of the $100 billion packaged‑food market would appear in red. Activists and influencers highlight the poorer ingredient quality of Indian versions of products like Fanta, KitKat and Maggi compared with overseas variants, and have faced legal push‑backs from the companies. The debate underscores the tension between public‑health goals, corporate profit motives, and emerging consumer demand for healthier, transparent food labeling in a rapidly affluent market

Zoho CEO Sridhar Vembu: The Trade‑off Between Metric‑Driven Predictability and Initiative‑Driven Chaos

Sridhar Vembu argues that highly metric‑driven organizations create a passive, instruction‑waiting culture that relies on flawless top‑down direction; any misstep by leadership can lead the firm into a dead end. In contrast, a culture that encourages seizing initiative is inherently chaotic, fostering internal disagreement, constant self‑critique, and a need for external market discipline to impose order. He warns that hybrid models attempting to combine both end up delivering the worst of each—losing predictability while stifling the energetic, argumentative drive needed for innovation. Netizens cite Amazon’s “Bias for Action” and “Disagree and Commit” principles as attempts to balance these forces, emphasizing the importance of honest feedback, separating performance metrics from personal assessment, and finding enough discipline to prevent chaos without killing initiative. Ultimately, Vembu suggests firms must choose: accept comfortable certainty and forgo initiative, or embrace initiative and tolerate discomfort
